## Concepts of legal thinking – Interrelationships

The fourth, and final, concept of legal thinking is **interrelationships**. For this concept you must consider the back-and-forth relationship between laws and society, in other words, how laws affect society and how society affects laws. In order to understand how the dynamics between law and society work you must understand the roles that the different branches of government have in developing laws and their role in citizens' daily lives. You must also be able to consider the roles and interactions that take place during a legal dispute, such as a trial, and how decisions made in these disputes may impact society. Finally, this concept requires you to consider how different groups are accommodated (or not) by the laws that govern our society.

## Judicial branch

In Learning Activity 1.3, you spent some time exploring the legal system and court structure in Ontario. These two elements make up the first branch of government, called the **judicial branch**.

### Responsibilities

1.  The judicial branch is responsible for interpreting the laws. If someone feels that they have been wronged by a law, they can apply to the courts to have the law interpreted. For example, Bill C-51 on anti-terror legislation states that “people who encourage or promote terrorism offences in general” are guilty under this law. Opponents of this bill stated that this was an unclear definition as individuals could be found guilty and sentenced to up to five years in prison, even if they did not commit the terrorist act they were planning. In this case, requests to the courts to interpret the bill were overruled and the description stayed the same, as the courts decided that it should remain as it is, in the interests of the people’s safety.
2.  It is up to the courts to apply and enforce the laws. Although parliament writes the laws, it is the courts that decide how to enforce them. For example, if you are pulled over by a police officer while driving and the officer gives you a ticket for speeding, it is important to keep in mind that the police officer isn’t the one who decides whether you are guilty or innocent – it is up to the courts to make that decision.
3.  It is also the role of the judicial branch to protect the rights of citizens. When you reviewed the idea of constitutional supremacy earlier in the course, you learned that any law that is passed in Canada must also respect the _Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms_. Canadians who feel that their rights are being violated can petition the courts to seek a solution.

### Who works in this branch?

The judicial branch is made up of anyone involved in Canada’s court system, from the Supreme Court justices in Ottawa to a court clerk in Windsor. All of these jobs are very specific, but each helps the Judicial branch carry out its responsibilities.

### Possible careers in this branch

These could include judges or justices, any court staff, or anyone involved in enforcing the law (for example, police officers).

## Executive branch

The executive branch is the branch of government that implements the laws that have been passed by parliament. This branch is responsible for finding a way to practically implement these new laws. Think about getting your driver’s licence, for example. Parliament first passes a law specifying what someone has to do in order to receive their licence. Then, the Ministry of Transportation has to figure out the “how” of implementing this law (in other words, answering questions such as, “Where will the testing centres be?” or “What skills will a new driver have to successfully demonstrate?”).

### Responsibilities

1.  The executive branch is responsible for the day-to-day operations of the government, and for ensuring that all policies created are implemented.
2.  They also need to ensure that any laws passed by parliament are implemented in a timely way, and in a manner that is fair and transparent to citizens.

### Who works in this branch?

**Note:** It is helpful to keep in mind that, for the most part, the executive branch of government is made up of employees sometimes referred to as “civil servants.” This title refers to the large number of jobs in the government that are not elected positions. If you are unsure whether a job is considered part of the executive branch of government, ask yourself whether the person in that role has been elected to the position or not. If the answer is no, and they don’t work in the judicial branch, you’ll know that they are a member of the executive branch.

The position of monarch, or their representative (the country's Governor General for federal matters, and a province's Lieutenant-Governor for provincial matters), is the most important position in the executive branch. In the next section of this learning activity you will explore how a law is created, but for the time being, note that a law can only be passed with the permission of the monarch or Governor General/Lieutenant-Governor.

At the federal level, there is the prime minister and the cabinet, while at the provincial level, there is the premier and the executive council.

## Legislative branch

For most people, the legislative branch is the one most easily associated with government. In Ontario, the provincial legislature, located at Queen’s Park in Toronto, represents the legislative branch. In your own community, however, at a local level, the city hall represents the legislative branch. What makes the legislative branch different from the others is that only people who are elected officials in the legislature (either at Parliament Hill in Ottawa, Queen’s Park, or your own city/town hall) are considered to be part of the legislative branch.

### Responsibilities

1.  The legislative branch is responsible for creating new laws, both in Ontario and Canada. As part of the process of creating a new law, the legislative branch also has an obligation to debate any newly proposed law to ensure that it is fair and will achieve what it sets out to do. (In the next section of this learning activity, you will explore the actual process involved in passing a law.)
2.  Additionally, should the legislative branch of government find that a law is not working as originally intended, it is their responsibility to amend or rescind the law.

### Who works in this branch?

Only persons directly involved in the creation of laws should be considered part of the legislative branch. For this reason, a Member of Provincial Parliament (MPP) would be considered a part of the legislative branch, while the Sergeant at Arms (an official who works inside the legislature) would be considered a part of the executive branch.

### Possible careers in this branch

These would include politicians and their immediate staff.

## Steps involved in passing a law

The graphic explains each of the steps involved in getting a potential law passed at the provincial level in Ontario. Take a few minutes now to review the different steps first, before reading about each step in more detail.

#### [Step 1: Idea](#accordion1-collapse1)

As an individual, you are unable to propose a law directly. In Ontario, the right to propose a law is restricted only to MPPs who have been elected to represent a certain geographic area, called a “riding.” So, where do MPPs get ideas from for proposed laws?

Some MPPs might be elected because they promise to introduce a proposed law dealing with an important issue in their riding. If, for example, their riding is close to the United States border, they might suggest that improvements be made to help speed up traffic flow across the border. If they are representing a rural riding, they might propose a law to provide specific support to farmers.

-   Alternatively, MPPs might propose a law based on a specific need that they have identified. Sabrina’s Law is an example of this type of law. Sabrina Shannon was a student in an Ontario school who experienced anaphylactic reactions (potentially life-threatening allergic reactions) to dairy products. While at school one day, Sabrina was unknowingly exposed to dairy products in the food from the school cafeteria, and passed away as a result. In response to her death, the Government of Ontario passed Sabrina’s Law, which forces school boards in Ontario to have plans in place to support students with anaphylaxis.
-   Finally, and far more infrequently, an MPP will propose a law based on feedback from someone who lives in their riding. In order for the MPP to take a proposed law seriously, it is helpful if the person from their riding has proof that there is a pressing need for the law. A citizen could meet this request by providing a signed petition containing thousands of names.

Once an MPP has an idea for a law, they work with their staff to draft the specific language of the law (in other words, outlining what the law will do, and how they will ensure that people understand the proposed law’s purpose).

#### [Step 2: First Reading](#accordion1-collapse2)

When an MPP has worked with their staff to refine their proposed law, they go to the Legislative Chamber and share their idea with their fellow MPPs. At this point, the proposed law is called a “bill.” The MPP explains the purpose of the bill, and the bill is translated into English and French, so that it can be made available to the general public.

#### [Step 3: Second Reading](#accordion1-collapse3)

The MPP proposing the bill debates it with other MPPs in the Legislative Chamber, and they vote on the bill’s idea, in principle. If the majority of MPPs vote to support the bill at this point, it moves on to Step 3. If, however, the majority does not vote in favour of the bill, any chance of it becoming a law ends and the bill is considered “dead.”

#### [Step 4: Committee examination](#accordion1-collapse4)

A committee of MPPs representing all political parties at Queen’s Park will then examine the bill if it has successfully passed a Second Reading. At the committee level, the MPPs will examine the bill in detail, clarify any misunderstandings, ask members of the public for their opinion, and make changes. At the end of this step, the committee members will vote on the new, refined bill. If the majority of committee members support the new bill, they will send it on to the Legislative Chamber.

#### [Step 5: Third Reading](#accordion1-collapse5)

The Third Reading is the last chance for a bill to become law. The following steps happen automatically: During a Third Reading, the bill is again read to the Legislative Assembly, and members are allowed to debate the bill in its updated form. At the end of a debate there is a vote, and if the bill successfully receives a majority of votes in its favour, it will become law. If it does not receive a majority of votes, the bill dies.

#### [Step 6: Royal Assent](#accordion1-collapse6)

Because of the Canadian legal tradition, formalities exist whereby a bill must be approved by the Lieutenant Governor before it can become a law. For the most part, this approval is automatic. Once the Lieutenant Governor signs the bill, it becomes a law. It might come into force on the day that it is “signed into law,” or it might come into effect at a later date.

![How a bill becomes law: First there is an IDEA. All laws start out as ideas. Next is a First Reading during which the purpose of bill is explained. Then there is a Second Reading during which the bill is debated in principle. Next is the Review by Committee. There may be public hearings and amendments considered. Next is the Report to House during which the committee reports bill with any amendments. Next is the Third Reading during which the bill is voted on for final approval. Finally, there is the Royal Assent during which the Lieutenant Governor signs the bill and it becomes a law!](./assets/img/clu3m_4.2.3.png)

## Differences between federal and provincial law making

The process for passing a bill in Ontario, which you’ve just learned about, is very similar to the process of creating a law in Ottawa. However, in addition to the steps just outlined (which occur in the House of Commons), a bill that is passed from the House of Commons must go through a very similar process in the Senate. Once a bill passes through the Senate, it is sent to the Governor General (as opposed to the Lieutenant Governor) for Royal Assent. Also, keep in mind that a bill passed in Ontario (or any province for that matter) only has legal authority in that province, while a bill passed in Ottawa can apply to the whole country, or can be limited to just one geographic region.

## Indigenous Self-government

Thus far in this learning activity your examination of the government and legal systems in Canada has focused on those established by the settlers of Canada. However, Indigenous Peoples practiced their own forms of government for thousands of years before the European settlers arrived. As there were many distinct Indigenous nations, each nation’s government differed, influenced by the diversity of cultural practices, spiritual beliefs, economy, social practices, and geography across these communities.

As you learned about in Learning Activity 1.4, treaties were used to establish partnerships between the settlers and Indigenous nations. However, over time these relationships deteriorated as settlers began to force their own laws, policies, and decisions upon the Indigenous nations, including the _Indian Act_ (which was briefly discussed in the previous learning activity), which “imposed a colonial governance system on First Nation communities where authority rested with the federal Minister.”

As part of the government of Canada’s work towards reconciliation between Indigenous and non-Indigenous Peoples, they are working with Indigenous nations to bring back and support self-governance amongst these communities. Explore the following document titled [Self-government](./assets/locker_docs/clu3m_01.05.301.html) to learn more about Indigenous self-government in Canada. Also, examine the following [Anishinabek Nation Education Agreement: Infographic](./assets/locker_docs/clu3m_01.05.100.html) from the federal government.
