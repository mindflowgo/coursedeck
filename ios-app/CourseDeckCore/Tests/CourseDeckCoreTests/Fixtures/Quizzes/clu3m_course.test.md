## Answers

01) C - (explanation) The lesson's comparison chart states that laws **must be passed by a government** — municipal, provincial/territorial, or federal — while rules "can be established by any group" (e.g., a hockey team or a school). Options A, B, and D are all characteristics of **rules** according to the chart: rules are enforced by the person or organization that created them, apply only to people who voluntarily choose to follow them, and carry consequences that are varied and often less serious.

02) D - (explanation) Applying the lesson's four criteria from the comparison chart: the hockey suspension was created by a **private organization**, applies only to people who **voluntarily joined** the association, is **enforced by the organization** (the coach), and carries a **less serious consequence** (a one-game suspension) — all hallmarks of a rule. The sidewalk by-law was **passed by a municipal government**, applies to **everyone** in the community, is **enforced by a government official** (the by-law officer), and carries a **severe consequence** (a $150 fine) — all hallmarks of a law. Options A and C fail because the lesson stresses that the source of authority (government vs. any group), scope of application, and enforcement body — not merely the existence of a consequence — determine whether a directive is a law.

03) A - (explanation) The lesson defines **legal significance** as the first of four concepts of legal thinking. It requires evaluating the importance of key legal principles, historical events, court decisions, and social forces by how greatly they have shaped our laws — explicitly stating that "the greater the impact on citizens' lives or existing legal structures, the greater the legal significance." Option B confuses significance with codification, C with enforcement frequency, and D with penalties; none of these capture the impact-based definition.

04) B - (explanation) The **rule of law** means that everyone in society — regardless of wealth, power, or position — is equally subject to the law; no one, including the government officials who create and administer the laws, is above them. This connects directly to the lesson's point that laws apply to everyone in a community and are enforced by government. Option A is incorrect because legal significance concerns a development's lasting impact on laws over time, not equal enforcement; C misapplies the reasonable-person standard; and D concerns which level of government or authority has legal power over an issue, not equality before the law.

05) A - (explanation) The lesson introduces **legal significance** as "the first of four concepts of legal thinking," explicitly naming the other three as **legal perspective**, **continuity and change**, and **interrelationships** — which students will encounter in later learning activities. Option B mixes the lesson's supporting principles (reasonability, rule of law) with terms from later units (precedent, jurisdiction); C lists criminal law terminology (mens rea, actus reus, stare decisis) from Unit 3; and D lists fairness concepts (equality, equity, justice, inclusiveness) from Unit 2's human rights material.

06) B - (explanation) The lesson's central conclusion is that **law is not static — it evolves to reflect changing societal values**, illustrated by the example of the Civil Marriage Act (2005) changing Canada's marriage laws. A legislature repealing an outdated law in response to shifted public values is the legal system functioning as intended. Option A contradicts the lesson directly; C is wrong because the Sunday-closing statute was a **law** (passed by government, applying to everyone) and was repealed by the legislature — the lawful law-making body — not "any group"; and D misapplies legal significance, which is measured by **impact** on citizens' lives and legal structures, not by a law's age or novelty.

07) government - (explanation) Per the lesson's comparison chart, laws are **enforced by government** — through police, by-law officers, and other officials — whereas rules are enforced only by the person or organization that created them. This is one of the key criteria distinguishing a law from a rule.

08) safe - (explanation) The chart states that rules are "created to teach people how to behave in a certain context," while laws are "created to ensure a **safe and peaceful society**." The lesson adds that laws should be non-discriminatory and ensure equal opportunity for all members of society.

09) everyone - (explanation) According to the comparison chart, laws "apply to **everyone** in a community, citizen or not," while rules "apply only to people who voluntarily choose to follow them." This universal scope is what separates, for example, a municipal by-law from a club's membership policy.

10) above - (explanation) The **rule of law** requires that every member of society be equally subject to the law regardless of their position or power — meaning no one, including government officials, is **above** the law. This principle supports the lesson's related requirement that laws be non-discriminatory and ensure equal opportunity, since the law must apply equally to all.
